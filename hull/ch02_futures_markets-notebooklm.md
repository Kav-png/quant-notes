# Futures Flashcards

## Card 1

**Q:** Term: Futures Contract

**A:** Definition: A standardised agreement traded on an exchange to buy or sell an asset at a certain time in the future for a specific price. Example: An investor enters a contract in March to buy 5,000 bushels of corn in July at 300 cents per bushel.

---

## Card 2

**Q:** How is a futures position 'closed out' before the delivery period?

**A:** The trader enters into a trade that is the exact opposite of the original transaction. Example: An investor with a long July gold position sells a July gold contract to cancel the obligation.

---

## Card 3

**Q:** What is the primary role of an exchange in specifying a new futures contract?

**A:** The exchange defines the exact nature of the agreement, including the asset, contract size, delivery location, and delivery period.

---

## Card 4

**Q:** In a futures contract, who typically chooses the specific grade of the asset or delivery location when alternatives are available?

**A:** The party with the short position (the seller) makes these selections when they are ready to deliver.

---

## Card 5

**Q:** Term: Initial Margin

**A:** Definition: The amount of funds that must be deposited in a margin account at the time a futures contract is entered into. Example: A broker requires $12,000 to be deposited to trade two gold futures contracts.

---

## Card 6

**Q:** The practice of adjusting a margin account at the end of each trading day to reflect gains or losses is known as _____.

**A:** Marking to Market (or Daily Settlement)

---

## Card 7

**Q:** Term: Maintenance Margin

**A:** Definition: The minimum balance that must be maintained in a margin account to ensure it does not become negative. Example: If the initial margin is $6,000, the maintenance margin might be set at $4,500.

---

## Card 8

**Q:** Term: Variation Margin

**A:** Definition: The extra funds an investor must deposit to bring a margin account back to the initial margin level after receiving a margin call. Example: An investor deposits $4,020 to restore an account balance from $7,980 to $12,000.

---

## Card 9

**Q:** How do margins protect investors and brokers against the possibility of default?

**A:** They ensure that funds are available to cover losses daily, preventing a large, unmanageable deficit from accumulating over the life of the contract.

---

## Card 10

**Q:** What is the key insight behind the convergence of the futures price to the spot price as the delivery period approaches?

**A:** Arbitrage opportunities exist if the prices differ; if the futures price is higher, traders will short futures and buy the asset, forcing the prices together.

---

## Card 11

**Q:** Term: Clearing House

**A:** Definition: A central intermediary that guarantees the performance of both parties in a derivatives transaction by becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. Example: The CME Group clearing house handles transactions for its member brokers.

---

## Card 12

**Q:** What is the difference between 'gross' and 'net' margining for a clearing house member?

**A:** Gross margining sums the long and short positions of all clients, while net margining offsets them against each other to calculate the required margin.

---

## Card 13

**Q:** Term: Open Interest

**A:** Definition: The total number of futures contracts outstanding in a market, which equals the total number of long positions (or short positions). Example: If there are 10,000 long positions currently held by various traders, the open interest is 10,000.

---

## Card 14

**Q:** How can trading volume be greater than the open interest on a particular day?

**A:** This occurs when 'day traders' enter and close out their positions within the same trading session, contributing to volume but not to the end-of-day outstanding contracts.

---

## Card 15

**Q:** Term: Normal Market

**A:** Definition: A market state where futures prices increase as the time to maturity of the contract increases. Example: Gold futures settling at $1,213 for June, $1,219 for September, and $1,227 for next June.

---

## Card 16

**Q:** Term: Inverted Market

**A:** Definition: A market state where the futures price of an asset decreases as the time to maturity of the contract increases. Example: A commodity futures price being lower for delivery in 12 months than for delivery in 3 months.

---

## Card 17

**Q:** What distinguishes a 'Stop-loss order' from a 'Limit order'?

**A:** A stop-loss order is executed at the best available price once a specific price is hit (to limit loss), whereas a limit order is only executed at the specified price or better.

---

## Card 18

**Q:** Term: Market-if-touched (MIT) order

**A:** Definition: An order that becomes a market order once a trade occurs at a specified price or one more favourable than the specified price. Example: An investor wanting to take profits if a price rises to a certain level uses an MIT order.

---

## Card 19

**Q:** Pitfall: Assuming that the initial margin for a futures contract is a 'sunk cost' or fee.

**A:** In reality, the margin account usually earns interest, and the investor can often deposit income-bearing securities (like Treasury bills) instead of cash.

---

## Card 20

**Q:** Pitfall: Confusing 'trading volume' with 'open interest' as measures of liquidity.

**A:** Volume counts the total number of trades made, while open interest counts only the total number of 'live' contracts that have not yet been closed or delivered.

---

## Card 21

**Q:** Pitfall: Believing that all futures contracts eventually result in the physical delivery of the underlying asset.

**A:** The vast majority of futures contracts are closed out (offset) prior to delivery; physical delivery is relatively rare.

---

## Card 22

**Q:** How does the 'marking to market' of futures contracts differ from the settlement of forward contracts?

**A:** Futures are settled daily, with gains or losses realised immediately, whereas forwards are usually settled only at the end of the contract's life.

---

## Card 23

**Q:** Term: Scalper

**A:** Definition: A type of speculator who watches for very short-term trends and attempts to profit from small price changes by holding positions for only a few minutes. Example: A trader on the exchange floor buying and selling contracts within seconds to capture tiny spreads.

---

## Card 24

**Q:** What is the primary responsibility of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC)?

**A:** The CFTC is a US federal body responsible for ensuring market integrity, preventing fraud, and approving new futures contracts to protect the public interest.

---

## Card 25

**Q:** Formula: Quoted price of a US Treasury Bill ($P$)

**A:** $P = \frac{360}{n} (100 - Y)$, where $n$ is the remaining life in days and $Y$ is the cash price.

---

## Card 26

**Q:** Formula: Price of a Eurodollar Futures Contract

**A:** $10,000 \times [100 - 0.25 \times (100 - Q)]$, where $Q$ is the quoted price.

---

## Card 27

**Q:** Formula: Cash received by the short position in a Treasury Bond delivery

**A:** $(Settlement\ Price \times Conversion\ Factor) + Accrued\ Interest$.

---

## Card 28

**Q:** How is a 'Cash Settlement' handled for futures on stock indices?

**A:** All outstanding contracts are declared closed on a specific day, and the final settlement price is set equal to the actual spot price of the index on that day.

---

## Card 29

**Q:** What is the function of the National Futures Association (NFA)?

**A:** Established in 1982, the NFA is a self-regulatory organisation that monitors trading, prevents fraud, and arbitrates disputes between individuals and its members.

---

## Card 30

**Q:** Term: Day Trader

**A:** Definition: A speculator who holds futures positions for less than one trading day, closing all positions before the market ends to avoid overnight risk. Example: A trader who buys crude oil futures at 10:00 AM and sells them at 3:30 PM the same day.

---

## Card 31

**Q:** Term: Position Trader

**A:** Definition: A speculator who holds futures positions for significant periods, hoping to profit from major market movements. Example: A fund manager holding a long gold position for six months based on macroeconomic forecasts.

---

## Card 32

**Q:** What is the 'haircut' in a collateralisation agreement?

**A:** A discount applied to the market value of non-cash securities deposited as margin to account for potential price volatility of the collateral itself.

---

## Card 33

**Q:** How does the 'Wild Card Play' in Treasury bond futures work?

**A:** The short position uses the time gap between the close of futures trading (2 PM) and the deadline for delivery notice (8 PM) to decide whether to deliver based on spot price moves.

---

## Card 34

**Q:** Term: Cheapest-to-Deliver Bond (CTD)

**A:** Definition: The specific bond that is most advantageous for the short position to deliver against a Treasury bond futures contract. Example: The seller chooses the bond that minimises the cost: $Quoted\ bond\ price - (Settlement\ price \times Conversion\ factor)$.

---

## Card 35

**Q:** In a futures market, what does it mean to 'corner the market'?

**A:** A group of investors takes a massive long position and controls the supply of the underlying asset, forcing short positions to pay high prices to close their contracts.

---

## Card 36

**Q:** Compare forward and futures contracts regarding 'Credit Risk'.

**A:** Forward contracts involve significant bilateral credit risk, while futures contracts mitigate this risk through the exchange clearing house and daily margin requirements.

---

## Card 37

**Q:** The price used to calculate daily gains and losses in a margin account is the _____ price.

**A:** Settlement

---

## Card 38

**Q:** Why are margin requirements for 'day trades' typically lower than for 'hedge' or 'speculative' trades?

**A:** Because positions are closed before the end of the day, there is no risk of price movements occurring while the market is closed.

---

## Card 39

**Q:** Term: Spread Transaction

**A:** Definition: A trade where an investor simultaneously takes a long position in one contract and a short position in a different contract on the same underlying asset. Example: Buying a July corn contract while selling a December corn contract.

---

## Card 40

**Q:** What is the key difference between an exchange-traded market and an over-the-counter (OTC) market?

**A:** Exchange markets trade standardised contracts through a central platform, while OTC markets involve private, non-standardised transactions between two parties.

---

## Card 41

**Q:** Term: Systemic Risk

**A:** Definition: The danger that the failure of one large financial institution will trigger a chain reaction of defaults, leading to a collapse of the entire financial system. Example: The near-collapse of the financial system following the Lehman Brothers bankruptcy in 2008.

---

## Card 42

**Q:** How did the 2007-2009 crisis change the regulation of the OTC derivatives market?

**A:** Governments introduced legislation requiring that standardised OTC transactions be cleared through central clearing houses, similar to exchange-traded futures.

---

## Card 43

**Q:** Concept: Marking to Model

**A:** Definition: The practice of valuing a derivative based on theoretical models rather than observed market prices, typically used for complex OTC instruments. Example: A bank using an internal interest rate model to value a highly tailored 20-year swap.

---

## Card 44

**Q:** Why is the maintenance margin usually set lower than the initial margin?

**A:** To reduce the frequency of margin calls caused by small, everyday price fluctuations, while still providing a buffer against default.

---

## Card 45

**Q:** If a trader does not meet a margin call, what action does the broker take?

**A:** The broker will immediately close out the trader's position by entering an offsetting trade in the market.

---

## Card 46

**Q:** Term: Dirty Price (of a Bond)

**A:** Definition: The actual cash price paid by a purchaser, which consists of the quoted (clean) price plus any accrued interest since the last coupon date. Example: Buying a bond quoted at 90.00 with $3.00 of accrued interest results in a cash payment of $93.00.

---

## Card 47

**Q:** In Eurodollar futures, what does a one-basis-point (0.01) change in the quote represent in monetary terms?

**A:** A $25 gain or loss per contract.

---

## Card 48

**Q:** Term: Board Order

**A:** Definition: Another name for a Market-if-touched (MIT) order, which waits for a price to be 'touched' before executing at the best current market price. Example: An order to buy if the price drops to 150 cents, but not as a limit order.

---

## Card 49

**Q:** How is the 'Conversion Factor' used in Treasury bond futures delivery?

**A:** It is a multiplier defined by the exchange to adjust the invoice price of different deliverable bonds so they are comparable to a standard 6% coupon bond.

---

## Card 50

**Q:** What is the key insight behind the 'AIG fiasco' regarding collateralisation?

**A:** AIG's AAA rating originally exempted it from posting collateral; when it was downgraded and losses mounted, it could not meet the sudden, massive collateral calls.

---

## Card 51

**Q:** Term: Central Clearing Party (CCP)

**A:** Definition: An entity that interposes itself between the two sides of a transaction, becoming the buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer to manage credit risk. Example: LCH.Clearnet acting as the CCP for interest rate swap markets.

---

## Card 52

**Q:** How does 'daily settlement' change the effective life of a futures contract?

**A:** Each day, the old contract is essentially closed out and rewritten at the new settlement price, meaning the total gain/loss is the sum of daily changes.

---

## Card 53

**Q:** Term: Limit Order

**A:** Definition: An instruction to trade only at a specified price or one that is more favourable to the investor. Example: An order to buy gold only if the price is $1,240 per ounce or less.

---

## Card 54

**Q:** What is the 'notice of intention to deliver' in futures markets?

**A:** A formal notification filed by the short position holder with the exchange, stating their readiness to deliver the asset according to contract terms.

---

## Card 55

**Q:** Why are most futures contracts closed out rather than settled by delivery?

**A:** Traders often use futures for hedging or speculation rather than sourcing physical goods, making cash offset more convenient and less costly than physical logistics.

---

## Card 56

**Q:** In the context of futures, what is a 'limit move'?

**A:** A price movement in a single day that equals the daily price limit set by the exchange, often resulting in a temporary halt in trading.

---

## Card 57

**Q:** Term: Forward Contract

**A:** Definition: A non-standardised private agreement to buy or sell an asset at a future date for a price agreed upon today, usually traded OTC. Example: A bank and a corporation agreeing to exchange USD for GBP in six months at a fixed rate.

---

## Card 58

**Q:** How does the 'clearing margin' differ from the 'customer margin'?

**A:** Customer margin is held by a broker for an investor, while clearing margin is held by the clearing house for a member broker to guarantee all their clients' trades.

---

## Card 59

**Q:** Term: Discretionary Order

**A:** Definition: A market order that allows the broker to delay execution to find a better price for the client. Example: An order to buy 10 contracts 'at the broker's discretion' throughout the morning.

---
