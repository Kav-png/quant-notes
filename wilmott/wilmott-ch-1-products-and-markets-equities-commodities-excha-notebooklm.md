# Finance Flashcards

## Card 1

**Q:** Term: Equities

**A:** Definition: Financial instruments representing ownership interest in a company, typically in the form of common stock. Example: Holding 100 shares of Microsoft (MSFT) which entitles the owner to a portion of the company's profits and voting rights.

---

## Card 2

**Q:** What is the primary effect of a stock split on a company's share price?

**A:** It reduces the share price proportionally to the split ratio so that the total market capitalisation remains unchanged.

---

## Card 3

**Q:** A company performs a three-for-one stock split. If the pre-split price was $150, what is the post-split price?

**A:** $50.

---

## Card 4

**Q:** Term: Dividends

**A:** Definition: A distribution of a portion of a company's earnings to its shareholders, usually in the form of cash. Example: A company paying $0.50 per share to all stockholders of record at the end of a financial quarter.

---

## Card 5

**Q:** Concept: Dividend Impact on Stock Price

**A:** When a stock goes 'ex-dividend', the share price typically drops by the amount of the dividend payment. Example: If a $100 stock pays a $2 dividend, the price theoretically falls to $98 on the ex-dividend date.

---

## Card 6

**Q:** Term: Commodities

**A:** Definition: Physical raw materials or primary agricultural products that can be bought and sold, often showing seasonal price effects and scarcity premiums. Example: Crude oil, gold, or orange juice traded on global markets.

---

## Card 7

**Q:** Term: Foreign Exchange (Forex/FX)

**A:** Definition: The global market for exchanging one national currency for another at an agreed-upon rate. Example: Exchanging US Dollars for British Pounds at a rate of $1.50 per £1.

---

## Card 8

**Q:** Concept: Consistent Cross Rates

**A:** Definition: The requirement that exchange rates between multiple currencies must align to prevent riskless profit opportunities. Example: If $1 = £0.5 and £1 = €1.2, then $1 must equal €0.6 to prevent arbitrage.

---

## Card 9

**Q:** Term: Stock Index

**A:** Definition: A weighted average or 'basket' of representative stocks used to measure the performance of a specific market or economy. Example: The FTSE 100 representing the 100 largest companies on the London Stock Exchange.

---

## Card 10

**Q:** Term: Forward Contract

**A:** Definition: A private agreement to buy or sell an asset at a specified future date for a price agreed upon today. Example: A farmer agreeing today to sell 1,000 bushels of wheat to a bakery in six months for £5 per bushel.

---

## Card 11

**Q:** Term: Delivery Price

**A:** Definition: The specific price set at the initiation of a forward contract that must be paid at maturity. Example: The £50 per share price agreed upon today for a transaction occurring in one year.

---

## Card 12

**Q:** Term: Futures Contract

**A:** Definition: A standardised, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell an asset at a future date, with gains and losses settled daily. Example: Trading oil futures on the NYMEX where the contract value is adjusted every day based on market moves.

---

## Card 13

**Q:** How do forward contracts and futures contracts differ in their cash flow timing?

**A:** Forwards involve a single cash payment at maturity, whereas futures require daily cash settlements known as marking to market.

---

## Card 14

**Q:** What is the initial value of a forward contract at the moment it is entered into?

**A:** Zero.

---

## Card 15

**Q:** Name: Forward Price (No Dividends)

**A:** Formula: $F = S(t) e^{r(T-t)}$, where $F$ is the forward price, $S(t)$ is the current spot price, $r$ is the risk-free interest rate, and $T-t$ is the time to maturity.

---

## Card 16

**Q:** How is the forward price formula $F = S(t)e^{r(T-t)}$ derived?

**A:** By creating a riskless portfolio involving a long forward, a short asset position, and cash in the bank, then applying the no-arbitrage principle.

---

## Card 17

**Q:** What is the key intuition behind the No-Arbitrage Principle?

**A:** If two different investment strategies yield the same guaranteed future cash flow, they must have the same cost today to prevent 'free lunches'.

---

## Card 18

**Q:** Term: Arbitrage

**A:** Definition: The simultaneous purchase and sale of an asset in different markets to exploit price discrepancies for a risk-free profit. Example: Buying gold in London for £1,200 and immediately selling it in New York for the equivalent of £1,210.

---

## Card 19

**Q:** Term: Going Short

**A:** Definition: The practice of selling an asset that one does not currently own, usually by borrowing it, with the intent to buy it back later at a lower price. Example: Borrowing 100 shares of a tech company to sell at $50, hoping to buy them back at $40.

---

## Card 20

**Q:** What occurs during 'marking to market' in a futures contract?

**A:** The profit or loss from the futures position is calculated daily, and the change in value is paid from one party to the other.

---

## Card 21

**Q:** Pitfall: What is a common misconception regarding the Forward Price and market expectations?

**A:** The forward price is often mistakenly thought to be the market's expected value of the asset at maturity, but it is actually determined by no-arbitrage and the risk-free rate.

---

## Card 22

**Q:** Pitfall: Under what specific condition are forward prices and futures prices theoretically identical?

**A:** They are only identical if interest rates are known in advance and constant; stochastic rates create a difference between the two.

---

## Card 23

**Q:** Pitfall: What institutional assumption is required for the no-arbitrage derivation of $F = S e^{r(T-t)}$ to hold?

**A:** It assumes there are no transaction costs, no taxes, and no restrictions on short-selling or borrowing at the risk-free rate.

---

## Card 24

**Q:** How does Wilmott distinguish between speculation and hedging?

**A:** Speculation is the deliberate taking of risk to profit from market views, whereas hedging is the avoidance or reduction of existing risk.

---

## Card 25

**Q:** Name: FX Forward Price

**A:** Formula: $F = S(t) e^{(r - r_f)(T-t)}$, where $r$ is the domestic risk-free rate and $r_f$ is the foreign risk-free rate.

---

## Card 26

**Q:** Name: Commodity Forward Price (with Storage and Yield)

**A:** Formula: $F = S(t) e^{(r + s - c)(T-t)}$, where $s$ is the storage cost and $c$ is the convenience yield.

---

## Card 27

**Q:** Term: Backwardation

**A:** Definition: A market condition where the forward or futures price of an asset is lower than the current spot price adjusted for interest. Example: Oil futures trading at $60 when the spot price plus carrying costs is $65.

---

## Card 28

**Q:** Term: Contango

**A:** Definition: A market condition where the forward or futures price of an asset is higher than the spot price adjusted for interest. Example: Gold futures trading at $2,100 when the current spot price plus interest is $2,050.

---

## Card 29

**Q:** What is 'convenience yield' in the context of commodity futures?

**A:** The non-monetary benefit or advantage of physically holding a commodity, which reduces the theoretical forward price.

---

## Card 30

**Q:** Term: Spot Price

**A:** Definition: The current market price at which an asset can be bought or sold for immediate delivery. Example: The price of gold shown on a dealer's screen for a transaction happening right now.

---

## Card 31

**Q:** In the hedged portfolio used to value a forward, what component cancels out the $S(T)$ term at maturity?

**A:** The short asset position cancels out the asset received from the long forward contract.

---

## Card 32

**Q:** A stock is $28.75 and the one-year interest rate is 4.92%. What is the no-arbitrage one-year forward price?

**A:** Approximately $30.20 (calculated as $28.75 \times e^{0.0492 \times 1}$).

---

## Card 33

**Q:** Why is the value of a futures contract always zero during its life?

**A:** Because any change in value is settled and paid out on a daily basis via marking to market.

---

## Card 34

**Q:** How do storage costs affect the forward price of a commodity?

**A:** They increase the forward price as they represent a cost of carry that must be compensated for in a no-arbitrage world.

---

## Card 35

**Q:** What is the purpose of 'standardisation' in futures contracts?

**A:** It promotes liquidity by ensuring that all contracts for a specific asset have the same quality, quantity, and delivery terms.

---

## Card 36

**Q:** If the sterling-euro rate is £1 = €1.41 and the dollar-euro rate is $1 = €0.77, what is the arbitrage-free dollar-sterling rate?

**A:** Approximately $1.83 per £1 (calculated as $1.41 / 0.77$).

---

## Card 37

**Q:** Term: Time Value of Money

**A:** Definition: The concept that a sum of money available now is worth more than the same sum in the future due to its potential earning capacity. Example: Receiving £100 today is preferred over £100 in a year because the money can earn interest in a bank account.

---

## Card 38

**Q:** According to Wilmott, why is the return of an asset more important than its absolute price change for modelling?

**A:** Returns allow for a consistent comparison between assets of different price levels, as investors care about percentage growth relative to capital invested.

---

## Card 39

**Q:** What is the 'delivery date' or 'maturity' of a forward contract?

**A:** The specified future date on which the buyer pays the delivery price and the seller provides the asset.

---

## Card 40

**Q:** How does the 'geometric random walk' differ from the 'arithmetic random walk' in asset modelling?

**A:** The geometric walk assumes changes are proportional to the current asset level (multiplicative), while the arithmetic walk assumes fixed absolute changes (additive).

---

## Card 41

**Q:** What is a 'lognormal' random walk in the context of stock prices?

**A:** A model where the logarithm of the asset price follows a normal distribution, ensuring that the asset price itself can never go negative.

---

## Card 42

**Q:** Term: Inflation-proof Bonds

**A:** Definition: Fixed-income securities where the principal and/or interest payments are adjusted according to a specified inflation index. Example: Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) in the US or Index-linked Gilts in the UK.

---

## Card 43

**Q:** What is the significance of 'liquid' instruments in financial markets?

**A:** Liquid instruments can be bought or sold quickly in large volumes without significantly affecting the market price.

---

## Card 44

**Q:** Why might a commodity futures contract specify a range of 'deliverable' grades?

**A:** Natural commodities vary in quality, so the contract defines acceptable standards to allow for physical settlement across different sources.

---

## Card 45

**Q:** What happens if the actual forward price $F$ in the market is greater than $S e^{r(T-t)}$?

**A:** An arbitrageur would sell the overvalued forward and buy the underlying asset (financed by a loan) to lock in a risk-free profit.

---

## Card 46

**Q:** What happens if the actual forward price $F$ in the market is less than $S e^{r(T-t)}$?

**A:** An arbitrageur would buy the undervalued forward and short-sell the underlying asset (investing the proceeds) to lock in a risk-free profit.

---

## Card 47

**Q:** In modelling, why is the 'drift' ($ \mu $) of an asset often scaled with time ($ dt $)?

**A:** Because the average expected return is assumed to be proportional to the length of the time horizon.

---

## Card 48

**Q:** How does the standard deviation of asset returns scale with time in a random walk model?

**A:** It scales with the square root of time ($ \sqrt{dt} $).

---

## Card 49

**Q:** Term: Fixed-income Securities

**A:** Definition: Investment instruments that provide a return in the form of fixed periodic interest payments and the eventual return of principal at maturity. Example: A corporate bond paying 5% interest annually until it matures in 10 years.

---

## Card 50

**Q:** What is the 'no free lunch' principle in finance also known as?

**A:** The principle of no arbitrage.

---

## Card 51

**Q:** How does a 'pegged' currency differ from a 'floating' currency?

**A:** A pegged currency has its value fixed against another currency or basket, while a floating currency's value is determined by market supply and demand.

---

## Card 52

**Q:** What is the primary motivation for speculators in the futures market?

**A:** To profit from their directional view of future price movements by taking on risk that others wish to avoid.

---

## Card 53

**Q:** How does Wilmott define the 'Premium' of an option?

**A:** The amount of money paid initially by the buyer to the seller for the rights granted by the option contract.

---

## Card 54

**Q:** What is 'Marking to Market' and where is it used?

**A:** It is the daily settlement of gains and losses, primarily used in exchange-traded futures contracts to manage credit risk.

---

## Card 55

**Q:** If a stock currently worth $100 pays a dividend of 5% ($DS$ where $D=0.05$), what is the post-dividend price?

**A:** $95.

---

## Card 56

**Q:** Why does the forward price formula not depend on the expected future value of the asset?

**A:** Because any expectation is already 'priced in' to the current spot price and the ability to hedge the contract eliminates the need for price predictions.

---

## Card 57

**Q:** Note: Wilmott (Math) vs Market Practice (Hull) on Forward/Futures

**A:** Wilmott treats them as identical if $r$ is known, but Hull notes that in practice, liquidity and institutional 'marking to market' requirements can cause small price divergences.

---

## Card 58

**Q:** What is a 'Short Asset Position'?

**A:** A position where an investor owes an asset to another party, typically created by selling a borrowed asset.

---

## Card 59

**Q:** How is the total return of a stock calculated according to the text?

**A:** The sum of the change in asset value plus any accumulated cash flows (dividends), divided by the original asset value.

---

## Card 60

**Q:** In the context of the binomial model, what does 'u' and 'v' represent?

**A:** $u$ is the factor by which the asset rises, and $v$ is the factor by which it falls during a single time step.
